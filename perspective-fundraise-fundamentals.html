    Finance for the Fundraising-Curious · 9 Oct 2026

    Fundraise Fundamentals: Four Friends

    What a fundraise is, explained with four friends and a house: shares, dilution, valuations, and which round you are raising.

    Sakshay UpadhyayInvestment Associate, Earlyseed Ventures

    In short

    Raising money means selling a small piece of your company. The pieces are called shares. The name of your round (seed, Series A and so on) depends on what you have already proven, not on how much you want.

    Four friends buy a house

    Aman, Bhavna, Chirag and Divya buy a house together for ₹1 crore. Each puts in ₹25 lakh. To keep things simple, they split the house into 100 shares, so each friend owns 25 shares, or 25%.

    One share is worth ₹1 crore ÷ 100 = ₹1,00,000. That is the share price.

    The ₹10 on the certificate

    Each share certificate says ₹10 on it. That is the face value, a fixed number for the company’s records. It never changes, even when the house becomes more valuable.

    The rest of what someone pays, here ₹99,990 a share, is called the share premium. Your balance sheet shows both after every round.

    A new friend joins

    Years later the friends need ₹30 lakh to renovate. A fifth friend, Esha, offers the money.

    The house is now worth ₹1.2 crore. That is its value before Esha’s money, the pre-money valuation. So one share now costs ₹1.2 crore ÷ 100 = ₹1,20,000, and Esha’s ₹30 lakh buys 25 new shares.

    Nobody sold Esha anything. The house made new shares for her, and her money went into the renovation. That is exactly what a startup fundraise is.

    Three things to notice:

    • Each friend went from 25% to 20%. That drop is called dilution.
    • Nobody lost money. Each friend’s 25 shares are now worth ₹30 lakh, more than the ₹25 lakh they put in.
    • The house is now worth ₹1.2 crore + ₹30 lakh = ₹1.5 crore. That is the post-money valuation.

    New shares, or someone else’s?

    When a company makes new shares for an investor, the money goes into the company. That is a primary issue, and it is what founders mean by “raising a round”.

    When an owner sells shares they already hold, the money goes to that owner, not the company. That is a secondary sale. If Aman sold 5 of his shares to Esha, the ₹6 lakh would go to Aman.

    Which round are you raising?

    Round names are a shorthand for how much you have proven, not how much you want. Answer honestly: the first “not yet” is roughly where you are.

    Raising a bigger round than your progress supports usually means a valuation you cannot grow into, and that makes the next round harder.

    Remember

    • Ask whether a valuation is pre-money or post-money. ₹30 lakh at ₹1.5 crore is 20% if that is post-money, but only about 16.7% if it is pre-money.
    • A smaller percentage is not a loss. If the money is used well, your shares are worth more.
    • Raising ₹10 crore does not make it a Series A. What you have proven does.

    Work it out for your own round with the pre-money and post-money calculator and the round finder.
