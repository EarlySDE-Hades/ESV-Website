In short

Indian health insurance pays when you land in hospital, not for the care that keeps you out. Insurers now reward customers for steps and sleep tracked on a wearable. The idea is good, but the rewards mostly reach people who are already healthy.

How it works today

You pay a premium every year. Nothing happens. Then you land in hospital, and the insurer pays. Everything before that, the doctor visits, tests and BP tablets, is mostly on you.

The squeeze

  • 10 crore Indians have diabetes, and one in three adults has high blood pressure.
  • Medical costs are projected to rise 11.5% in 2026, above the 9.8% global average.
  • People with diabetes or high blood pressure often pay 25 to 50% more for cover.

Heart disease and high blood pressure are now the biggest drivers of claims: slow, largely preventable conditions, in exactly the part of the journey insurance ignores.

The idea: pay people to stay healthy

Since 2020, IRDAI has let insurers reward healthy habits with points, renewal discounts and vouchers. On one popular plan, walking 10,000 steps a day, sleeping well and logging meals can earn back part of the premium: up to 100% in theory, 30 to 50% for a fit person in practice.

The gaps

  • The rewards miss the people driving the claims. A fit 28-year-old with a smartwatch earns them. A 55-year-old with diabetes, who already pays more, mostly does not.
  • Your wrist says more than you think. Heart rate, sleep and location can hint at illness, stress or a pregnancy, and the company receiving the data decides how risky you are. India’s new data protection rules require clear consent, but most people tap “I agree” without reading.
  • The evidence is thin. A third of wearable buyers stop using them within six months, and Indian insurers publish little on whether these programmes reduce claims.

What would fix it, and where startups fit

  • Reward outcomes, such as blood sugar or blood pressure in range, not steps.
  • Affordable, trusted devices built for Indian patients: glucose monitors, connected BP machines, ECG wearables.
  • Policies that cover the care in between: teleconsults, tests and medicines.
  • Tools that help insurers handle consent and keep health data safe.
  • Running wellness programmes for insurers, which most do not want to build themselves.
