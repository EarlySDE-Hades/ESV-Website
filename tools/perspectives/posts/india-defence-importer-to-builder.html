In short

India is still the world’s second largest arms importer. But it is building more at home than ever: defence production reached a record ₹1.78 lakh crore in FY26. Geopolitics supplied the urgency, and policy reforms changed the economics.

Where India started

India bought 8.2% of the world’s arms imports in 2021 to 2025, second only to Ukraine. Russia’s share of what India buys has fallen from 70% to 40%, with France (29%) and Israel (15%) making up most of the rest.

What changed: two triggers

  • The war in Ukraine exposed supply risk. In 2023, the Air Force told Parliament a major delivery from Russia would not arrive. Spares for its core fighter fleet and the last S-400 squadrons were delayed. Relying on one foreign supplier became an operational risk.
  • Modern wars changed what matters. Cheap drones, counter-drone systems, electronic warfare and ammunition now decide outcomes as much as fighter jets, and Indian companies can build these quickly and at lower cost.

What changed: the rules

  • Import bans. Positive Indigenisation Lists name items that must now be bought from Indian makers, over 5,400 items so far. That guaranteed demand Indian industry never had.
  • 75% for Indian companies. Three quarters of the capital acquisition budget, about ₹1.39 lakh crore in FY27, is reserved for Indian firms.
  • Foreign investment. Allowed up to 74% automatically and 100% with approval, which makes joint ventures with foreign technology partners possible.

The proof, and the new rulebook

In May 2025, many of the headline weapons in Operation Sindoor were Indian, from Akash and BrahMos to drones from young private companies. Then, in February 2026, a rewritten Defence Acquisition Procedure:

  • made “design and build in India” the default route,
  • fast-tracked purchases under ₹75 crore, cutting the cycle from about seven years to 18 to 24 months,
  • and compensated vendors who clear trials but lose the contract, up to ₹10 crore.

Faster contracts mean faster payback, which makes the case for defence startups far stronger.

The bottom line

India will keep importing high-end platforms for years. But the direction is set. The next test is whether Indian companies can move beyond assembly and own the core technology inside the weapons. The targets: over ₹2 lakh crore of production this year, and ₹3 lakh crore by 2029.
