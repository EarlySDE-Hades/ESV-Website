    Same Money, Different Paper: Esha's Options

    The same ₹30 lakh can buy very different deals. What a liquidation preference is, and the kinds of shares and loans investors use.

    Sakshay UpadhyayInvestment Associate, Earlyseed Ventures

    In short

    How much an investor gives you is only half the deal. The other half is what they get in return. The same ₹30 lakh can buy plain shares, shares that are paid back first, or a loan that turns into shares later. Read that part of the deal as carefully as the valuation.

    Esha asks for one more thing

    In Part 1, Esha put ₹30 lakh into the house for 20% of it. Before she pays, she adds one condition:

    “If we ever sell the house for less than we paid, I get my ₹30 lakh back before anyone else is paid.”

    That one sentence is what this whole article is about.

    When the house sells for less

    Say the house has to be sold for just ₹75 lakh.

    With plain shares, everyone takes 20%, so everyone gets ₹15 lakh. Esha loses half of her ₹30 lakh.

    With Esha’s condition, she is paid first. She takes her ₹30 lakh, and the four friends share the ₹45 lakh left, ₹11.25 lakh each.

    The house sells for ₹75 lakh
    WhoPlain sharesEsha paid first
    Esha₹15 lakh₹30 lakh
    Each friend₹15 lakh₹11.25 lakh

    The condition did not create money. It moved ₹15 lakh from the friends to Esha, in the one case where there was not enough to go around.

    This is called a liquidation preference. The usual size is 1x: the investor gets back one times what they put in. It costs founders nothing when things go well, and a lot when they do not.

    When the house sells for more

    Now say the house sells for ₹3 crore. Taking just her ₹30 lakh back would be a bad deal for Esha. So her shares can convert into ordinary shares, and she takes her 20% instead: ₹60 lakh.

    The line is the ₹1.5 crore price she bought in at. Below it she takes her money back. Above it she converts.

    The kinds of paper you will see

    • Equity shares. Plain ownership. What founders usually hold. No protection, last in the queue.
    • CCPS (compulsorily convertible preference shares). Exactly Esha’s deal: paid first on the way down, turned into ordinary shares on the way up. The most common choice for investors in Indian rounds.
    • CCDs (compulsorily convertible debentures). A loan that must turn into shares later. Often used for quick money between two rounds. Until it converts, it is a loan on your books and may carry interest.
    • NCDs and OCDs. Loans that never turn into shares, or only if the lender chooses. This is venture debt: the lender wants to be repaid, not a share of your growth.
    • iSAFE. Money now, shares at your next round, usually at a discount. Fast and simple. You still give up the shares, you just find out how many later.

    Remember

    • Do not look only at the valuation. A higher valuation with tough terms can leave you worse off than a lower one with a plain 1x.
    • Ask if the preference is “participating”. If it is, the investor takes their money back and a share of what is left.
    • Push back on anything above 1x. At 2x, Esha would take ₹60 lakh of a ₹75 lakh sale.
    • An iSAFE is not free money. Work out how many shares it will cost you at the next round before you sign.

    Try your own term sheet in the exit waterfall.

    This is general information, and the examples and tools are indicative only. It is not financial advice. Always check with a legal advisor before you sign anything, and check the numbers with your CA.
